Project 01 · Credit risk

Digital lending credit risk in SQL

How risky is the loan book, which segments drive defaults, what loss should we expect on active loans, and which approval rule would have made the most money?

20.2%default rate, mature loans
55.6%default rate when instalment > 60% of income
Ksh 2.19Mexpected loss, 10.9% of exposure
+Ksh 5.5Mbest rule vs approving everyone

What I did

  1. Ran seven data quality checks in one SQL query: duplicates, invalid scores, orphan loans and repayments, status rules. All passed.
  2. Avoided the right-censoring trap. Loans under 180 days old default at 16.0% only because they haven't had time to fail, so all risk figures use the 485 mature loans.
  3. Broke default rates down by product, credit-score band, instalment-to-income ratio and occupation.
  4. Tracked monthly vintages with a SQL window function for a 3-month rolling default rate, and computed PAR30 and PAR90 from the repayment schedule.
  5. Estimated expected loss on the active book as PD × LGD × EAD, by product.
  6. Priced three approval rules in shillings on the mature cohort, counting interest earned against principal lost.
Approval ruleApprovedBad rateNet value
Approve everyone100.0%20.2%Ksh 4.06M
Decline credit score under 55081.6%15.4%Ksh 5.74M
Decline instalment over 60% of income75.9%9.0%Ksh 9.58M
Decline either63.9%6.8%Ksh 8.37M

Findings and recommendations

  • Add an affordability cap: decline instalments above 60% of income. It more than doubles net value while approving three in four applicants.
  • Don't stack it with the score cut-off. Declining either loses more good business than it saves.
  • Asset Finance defaults most (36.0%) and carries most of the expected loss, Ksh 1.53M of 2.19M. Review its terms first.
  • Report risk on mature loans only, and show vintages so a bad month like 2025-07 (50% default) is caught early.

The six SQL queries are on GitHub in credit_risk_queries.sql, with the database they run against.

Project 02 · Machine learning

Predicting loan default

Can we rank applicants by default risk, and where should the approval cut-off sit to make the most money?

0.84test AUC, logistic regression
0.56KS statistic
72%of defaults caught at the cut-off
−Ksh 1.59M → +462ktest-set profit, approve-all vs model

What I did

  1. Built one feature row per loan in SQL, including transaction activity in the 90 days before disbursement.
  2. Caught leakage before modelling: days_past_due alone gave a perfect AUC of 1.0 because it's only known after the outcome. I removed it and status.
  3. Kept only loans observed for at least 270 days (404 of 560) so unfinished loans aren't counted as good.
  4. Set baselines every model had to beat: a constant prediction (AUC 0.50) and the credit score alone (AUC 0.74).
  5. Compared logistic regression with gradient boosting using cross-validation. The simpler logistic model won on the held-out test set.
  6. Chose the approval cut-off by expected profit on training folds, applied it once to the test set, and checked approval rates by gender and customer segment.

Findings and recommendations

  • The instalment-to-income ratio is the strongest predictor, which matches Project 01's finding.
  • At the profit-based cut-off the model declines 38 of 122 test applicants and catches 18 of 25 defaults, turning a Ksh 1.59M loss into a Ksh 462k profit.
  • Women are approved at 0.89 times the rate of men, although both groups default at about the same rate (21% and 20%) in this sample. That passes the common four-fifths rule, but the gap should be explained before any use. The Mass segment's 17% approval rate (its loans default at 54%) needs a policy decision.
  • With only 404 usable loans, retrain and re-check once more loans mature.

The sample is small and synthetic, and active loans are treated as good, so these scores show method rather than production performance.

Project 03 · Fintech

M-Pesa personal finance analytics

Where does the money go, is the account holder saving, which payments look unusual, who gets paid most, and what should next quarter's spending look like?

26.4%savings rate over 24 months
5,045clean rows of 5,085 raw
12 / 12anomalies caught, 0 false alarms
27%lower forecast error than naive

What I did

  1. Read the statement as text, parsed dates and money explicitly, and accounted for every row: 25 duplicates and 15 rejects with reasons, 5,045 clean rows. Checked each running balance against the row amounts.
  2. Categorised transactions from the Details text with 16 ordered keyword rules. A naive rule list put 192 matatu fares under Savings because "Sacco" appears in both; rule order fixed it.
  3. Split money into income, saving and spending: Ksh 3.43M in, Ksh 2.53M spent, a 26.4% savings rate, with 3 of 24 months spending more than came in.
  4. Scored 74 payees on recency, frequency and money (RFM). 23 core partners take 55% of all money paid out.
  5. Flagged unusual payments with z-scores within each category, then tuned the rule to cut false alarms from 41 to 0.
  6. Fitted a spending trend, back-tested it against a naive "repeat the last three months" baseline, and forecast the next quarter with a range.

Forecast, tested before trusted

Spending rises by about Ksh 849 a month, and December runs 1.65 times the trend. On held-out months the trend's average error was Ksh 11,362, against Ksh 15,666 for the naive baseline. The forecast for January to March 2026 is Ksh 87,600 to 89,300 a month, with a range of roughly ±Ksh 14,000.

Findings and recommendations

  • Cash withdrawals are the largest category at 27%, so a quarter of spending is invisible. Tag cash and person-to-person transfers for one month.
  • Budget December as 1.65 times a normal month and set money aside in October and November.
  • Automate a Sacco transfer on salary day to smooth out the months with negative saving.
  • Send any payment with a high log z-score and over Ksh 10,000 to a confirm-or-dispute list.

The statement is synthetic and template-driven, so it shows method, not a real person's finances. Real statements contain other people's names and numbers, which should be masked before sharing.

Project 04 · Machine learning

Customer segmentation for a SACCO

Which natural groups of members exist, and what should the SACCO do differently for each?

4segments, each with an action
0.465silhouette score, against −0.03 for random groups
1.00stability across random starts (ARI)
69%of the variation shown on one 2-D map

What I did

  1. Built seven behaviour measures per member and log-transformed the skewed money and count columns, cutting the skew in average balance from 2.12 to 0.26.
  2. Scaled every measure so that no single unit (shillings against app logins) dominates the distances between members.
  3. Compared k-means for 2 to 8 groups. Four groups gave the best silhouette score, 0.465, against −0.03 for randomly assigned labels.
  4. Tested stability: other random starts and a bootstrap model gave the same four groups (adjusted Rand index 1.0).
  5. Checked the groups on a PCA map and wrote a profile in shillings and counts, with one action and one metric for each segment.
SegmentMembersTypical member (median)ActionTrack
Power users17146 transactions a month, Ksh 23,500 balance, 39 app loginsProtect with fast support and fee tiers; cross-sell merchant accountstransactions per month, churn
Savers1497 transactions a month, Ksh 88,700 balance, 40 months as a memberOffer fixed-deposit or goal-savings products; consider loans against savingsbalance, savings uptake
Agent-reliant15012 transactions a month, 90% through agents, 2 app loginsAgent-assisted onboarding to the app; keep agent float healthyapp logins, agent share
Light users1805 transactions a month, Ksh 2,900 balance, 7 months as a memberA first-loan offer or airtime bonus for a second transaction30-day reactivation

Findings and recommendations

  • Savers hold by far the largest balances but rarely transact: the natural audience for deposit and goal-savings products, and for loans secured on savings.
  • Agent-reliant members barely use the app. Moving them to it cuts agent costs, but only with agent-assisted onboarding.
  • Light users are the largest and newest group, so their first months decide whether they stay.
  • Treat each segment as a hypothesis: test its action on a sample of members before rolling it out.

Synthetic customers separate far more cleanly than real ones (the segments match the generator's hidden types 99.6%). Real members form a blurry continuum, so expect lower silhouette scores and review segments every quarter.

Project 05 · Insurance

Insurance data quality and claim frequency

Which insurance records can be trusted, what is wrong with the rest, and what do the trusted records say about claim frequency by product?

1,168clean policies of 1,219
222claims accepted of 283
16validation rules
3.15%premium overstatement caught

What I did

  1. Wrote validation rules as data: a frozen Rule class with an ID, a test and a severity, so rules can be added or reviewed without touching the pipeline.
  2. Split policies into clean and quarantined (15 duplicates, 36 quarantined), then joined claims only to clean policies.
  3. Caught 10 re-keyed duplicate claims and 15 orphan claims that an ID check alone would miss.
  4. Showed the join trap: joining claims back to policies before aggregating counts premium twice, overstating it by 3.15%. Aggregating first and joining one-to-one fixes it.
  5. Computed frequency, severity and loss ratio with intervals, and delivered an exception workbook (scorecard, 61 policy and 53 claim exceptions) for the operations team.
ProductFrequency / 100 policy-yrsAvg claim (Ksh)Loss ratio
Medical33.121,75916.0%
Motor Private21.658,71943.5%

Findings and recommendations

  • Make policy and claim IDs mandatory at export, and add a re-keyed duplicate check at claims intake.
  • Route claims outside the cover period or above the sum insured to manual review.
  • Report frequency with intervals, not rankings. County rates overlap heavily, so no county should be singled out.
  • Only 83.8% of true claims survive validation, so every rate here is a lower bound.

What I did

Project 07 · Macroeconomics

Kenya against six regional peers

How has income per person grown, how stable have prices been, and how does Kenya compare with Uganda, Rwanda, Tanzania, Ethiopia, Nigeria and South Africa?

US$1,941GDP per person in 2023, up from US$415 in 2000
4.6%real growth a year, 2nd of 7
8.0%mean inflation, 3rd highest
27.7%peak inflation, 2009

What I did

  1. Loaded four public files (about 59,000 rows) and kept countries only, using ISO codes and logging every dropped row.
  2. Merged on country and year with a one-to-one check: 10,920 rows in and 10,920 out, with population totals reconciled.
  3. Computed GDP per person, real growth (deflated by US prices), five-year blocks and rolling inflation volatility.
  4. Checked outliers before ranking: Uganda's 85.4% inflation in 2009 was excluded from the peer comparison only.
  5. Turned the result into a lending question: what a fixed-rate loan earns once inflation is taken out.

Lending illustration

A fixed 16% loan (an illustrative rate) would have earned a real yield between −11.7 and +15.1 points across the period, falling below zero in two years. Keeping a 6-point real margin needs about 12.6% nominal at median inflation and 19.6% at the 90th percentile.

Findings and recommendations

  • Price loans for inflation volatility, not only average inflation.
  • Stress-test affordability in high-inflation years: 4 of 24 years were at 10% or more.
  • Compare countries on a shared time window. Over 2010–2023 Kenya's volatility rank moves to 5th of 7.
  • CO₂ per dollar of GDP fell 76% since 2000. Treat it as background context, not a credit signal.

These are questions to test with current data, not lending decisions.

Project 08 · Operations

Workshop production and OEE

Output is slipping and the scrap bin is filling. Which machine, shift and cause is behind it, and what should the owner fix first?

83.3%plant OEE: availability × performance × quality
Millthe bottleneck, and the lowest OEE at 79.7%
2×Mill downtime after its change week
5.4%Grinder scrap on nights, against 1.4–1.6% by day

What I did

  1. Cleaned a hand-typed log: found 24 spellings of five machine names and repaired them, standardised shift names, removed 12 duplicates and set aside 19 impossible rows with reasons (2,336 clean rows of 2,367).
  2. Measured OEE (overall equipment effectiveness) for each machine: the share of planned time it ran, its speed against the standard cycle, and the share of good pieces.
  3. Found the bottleneck from good output per shift against what each machine should make: the Mill.
  4. Ranked downtime causes in a Pareto chart: setups and tool changes are 60% of all lost minutes.
  5. Flagged any machine running above 1.5 times its own downtime baseline for a sustained period: the Mill, from the week of 1 September 2025.
  6. Costed scrap in shillings by machine and shift.
MachineOEEScrap rateShare of scrap loss
Saw86.2%0.62%10.5%
Lathe83.3%1.25%10.6%
Mill (bottleneck)79.7%1.49%11.6%
Drill84.0%1.83%26.9%
Grinder82.3%2.79%40.4%

Findings and recommendations

  • From 1 September the Mill lost 73 minutes a shift to downtime instead of 37, and tool changes rose from 28% to 58% of it. Inspect its cutters, inserts and coolant now.
  • Protect the bottleneck: do setups and tool changes between batches and keep spare tooling at the Mill, because a minute lost there is lost for the whole line.
  • Review first-piece inspection, lighting and handover on the Grinder's night shift, the largest single source of scrap cost.
  • Alert when any machine runs above 1.5 times its own downtime baseline for three weeks, and use drop-down lists for machine and shift names at data entry.

The data is synthetic, with the Mill problem and the night-shift problem planted so the method has something to find. Shilling values per piece are illustrative.
